The Golden Era of Goalkeeper Kits: 90s Shirts That Went Wild

Kit Finder November 2026 6 min read

Look at a recent goalkeeper kit: plain colours, the same cut, the same template. Clean, sensible, and — in our opinion — a bit boring.

Recent goalkeeper shirts. Top row: AC Milan, Atlético Madrid, West Ham. Bottom row: Sevilla, Arsenal, Real Madrid.

It wasn't always like this. In the 1990s, goalkeepers wore some of the loudest shirts the game has ever seen: swirls, cartoons, neon. Shirts so bold that today they're some of the most sought-after pieces in vintage kit collecting. But to understand why they feel so wild, you have to start with how little freedom keepers once had.

Before the 90s

A handful of colours

For decades, goalkeepers were limited to a short list of colours: green, blue, scarlet or white. In international matches they could wear yellow or black, following a 1921 ruling by the International Football Association Board. The rules were only relaxed in the 1970s.

Green was the favourite, mostly because very few teams wore green as their first-choice colour — so the keeper stood out from his team-mates. Another detail that sounds strange today: until the mid-1990s, goalkeepers usually wore the same shorts and socks as the rest of their team.

Then the rulebook loosened, the kit makers got creative, and the keepers got louder.

The 1990s, top row: Roma, Juventus, Tottenham. Bottom row: Manchester United, Palmeiras. Look at the sponsors too — Sony MiniDisc, Sharp Viewcam, Hewlett Packard, Parmalat.
01 / 03

Bodo Illgner × Germany, Italia 90

adidas goalkeeper shirt, early 1990s: swirls in black, pink and yellow.

Germany won the 1990 World Cup, and their goalkeeper Bodo Illgner did it in a swirling, striped adidas shirt that looked like nothing else at the tournament. He wore it in at least three colourways, including a blue version against Colombia.

Nobody was designing goalkeeper shirts like this in 1990, and it set the tone for the decade: from here on, keepers' kits would only get bolder.

02 / 03

Carlos Navarro Montoya × Boca Juniors, 1995-96

Boca Juniors goalkeeper shirt, 1995-96: the cartoon truck.

If Illgner's shirt was bold, Montoya's was a cartoon. The Boca Juniors keeper wore a baggy yellow shirt with a cartoon truck across the chest, with Montoya himself drawn behind the wheel. Blue and pink splashes, white stars, psychedelic sleeves, shorts and socks — the whole kit went all in. Boca also had a white version for colour clashes.

Why a truck? Why not.

That's more or less how Planet Football summed it up, and it's the best explanation you'll find. The shirt needs no backstory. It simply exists, and that's why people love it.

03 / 03

Jorge Campos × Mexico, Pumas & ACA Sport

Aca Sport "Acapulco Surfer" goalkeeper shirt: the neon style Campos made famous.

No keeper is more linked to loud shirts than Jorge Campos. The Mexican goalkeeper designed most of his own kits and even ran his own brand, ACA Sport — named after his hometown, Acapulco. Umbro and Nike partnered with him, putting their labels on his designs.

The result: neon colours and dazzling geometric patterns, including a fluorescent, psychedelic kit at the 1994 World Cup. The press often called it flashy. Campos has said the colours came from growing up in Acapulco as a surfer — he liked expressing himself, and it wasn't about putting strikers off.

His 1990s shirts, from Mexico to Pumas UNAM and later LA Galaxy, are some of the most recognisable goalkeeper kits ever made.

The other way to stand out

Not every keeper wanted to be the loudest man on the pitch. In the early 1990s the Premier League's referees started wearing green, so keepers in green had to choose something else — and a lot of black goalkeeper kits followed. Everton's Neville Southall wore black and had a simple reason: "I thought a striker would have to look twice to make sure I was there."

Loud or quiet, the point is the same. For the first time, goalkeepers had real choice, and the shirts they chose tell a story that outfield kits rarely do.
